7 travel deals worth booking — and 5 that aren't

A deal is not a discount. It's a good price for something you'd buy anyway. Here's the four-question test we use, plus the seven deals that usually pass and the five that usually don't.

Every travel company calls its offer a deal. The word has been stretched so far that it now means “thing we would like you to buy.” A $99 hotel room that normally costs $129 is a deal. A $99 hotel room that normally costs $89, with a $30 resort fee, is not.

The difference isn't cynicism. It's arithmetic. These four questions catch almost every bad deal before you get to the payment page.

The four-question test

  1. What's the real baseline? Not the “was” price on the page — the actual price for the same route, room, or tour in the same season, from a source that isn't trying to sell you this specific deal.
  2. What's the total cost? Add bags, seat fees, taxes, resort fees, transfers, parking, and the compulsory breakfast. Compare that number to the baseline's total.
  3. What flexibility am I giving up? Nonrefundable, no changes, no bags, middle seat, 4 a.m. departure. A deal that costs you a day of flexibility is often not a deal at all.
  4. Would I book this at full price if I needed to go? If the answer is no, you're not buying a trip. You're buying a discount.
The 30-second test

If you can't explain why the price is low — off-season, unsold inventory, a new route, a mistake, a package with a constraint — assume the “deal” is marketing. Genuine discounts have a reason.

7 deals worth booking

1. Mistake fares — if you can afford to lose

A fare 60–80% below baseline, booked direct, with flexible dates, is one of the best uses of money in travel. The catch is real: the airline can cancel and refund. Read our full mistake-fare guide before you book one.

2. Shoulder-season hotels

The two weeks before peak season are usually the best value in travel. The weather is close enough, the crowds are thinner, and the rates haven't caught up. Santorini in late May, the Amalfi Coast in early June, ski towns in September — same rooms, 30–50% less.

3. Repositioning cruises

When ships move between regions — the Mediterranean to the Caribbean, Alaska to Asia — they sail with unsold cabins. The one-way routes are cheap, the days at sea are plentiful, and the price per night can beat a mid-range hotel. You need flexible dates and a tolerance for sea days.

4. Award redemptions when cash prices spike

Points are most valuable when the cash price is high. A hotel room at $320 a night is a great redemption at 15,000 points; the same room at $110 is not. Check the cash rate first, then decide whether to use points. Our beginner's guide covers the math.

5. Small-group tours at early-bird pricing

Reputable operators discount early bookings to lock in departure numbers. You get the same guide and itinerary for 10–20% less, in exchange for committing months ahead. Only worth it if the operator's cancellation policy is reasonable and the group size is genuinely small.

6. Green-season safaris

The “green season” in southern Africa — roughly November to March — brings rain, dramatic skies, newborn animals, and rates around half of peak. Some camps close for the wettest weeks; the ones that stay open are excellent value. Check what “green season” means for the specific camp and region.

7. Packages with transfers in expensive destinations

In places where a transfer costs $300 and a la carte rooms are marked up, a package that includes the boat, the transfers, and the meals can genuinely beat DIY. Compare the total, not the room rate. This is one of the few cases where a package is the smarter buy.

5 deals that usually aren't

1. Opaque bundles

“Save up to 40% — hotel revealed after booking.” The savings are usually modest, the room category is the worst one, and you have no recourse if the location is wrong for you. The uncertainty is the product. Skip it unless the discount is enormous and the dates are truly flexible.

2. “Free” nights with resort fees

A free-night certificate that comes with a $45 resort fee, a $30 parking charge, and a $25 “destination fee” is still a discount — just a much smaller one than advertised. Add the mandatory charges before you celebrate.

3. Basic economy when you need flexibility

Basic economy is a legitimate product for a traveler with one small bag and fixed plans. It's a bad deal for anyone who needs a checked bag, a seat assignment, or the ability to change a date. By the time you add those back, you've often paid more than the main-cabin fare.

4. Last-minute holiday air

The week before Christmas and the days around Thanksgiving are the least discountable inventory in the industry. If you see a “sale” for those dates, compare it to the normal fare — it's often just the normal fare with a red banner.

5. Timeshare and vacation-club pitches

The “free” three-night stay in exchange for a 90-minute presentation is not free. It costs you a morning, a high-pressure sales pitch, and sometimes a deposit you didn't plan to make. The math rarely works in your favor unless you genuinely want the product.

The 30-second score

Score each question from 1 to 5, then add them up:

Question1 point5 points
Is the baseline real?“Was” price onlyVerified against normal market price
Is the total cost low?Fees erase the discountTotal beats the realistic alternative
Is the flexibility acceptable?No changes, no bags, bad timesReasonable terms for your plans
Would you book it anyway?Only because it's cheapYes — the trip fits your life

16–20: book it. 11–15: worth a closer look, but read the terms. 10 or below: it's a discount, not a deal. Walk away.

How we apply it

Every deal in the Sunday brief goes through this test before it ships. We check the baseline, price the total, read the cancellation terms, and ask whether we'd book it ourselves. If it fails, it doesn't go out — even if the affiliate commission would be lovely.

That's the whole philosophy: a deal should make your trip better or cheaper. If it does neither, it's just marketing with a countdown timer.
